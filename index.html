Water Leak Bill Adjustment: How to Get Relief After a Leak

You open the water bill and it's triple the normal amount. Then you remember the toilet that ran for two weeks, or the irrigation line that split underground in July. Before you just pay it, know this: most water utilities offer a water leak bill adjustment — a one-time credit for bills inflated by a repaired leak. Here's how to claim yours.

Does Your Utility Offer Leak Adjustments?

In our area, the answer is usually yes — with conditions. Most utilities serving Redmond allow one adjustment per account every year or two, and it typically covers the sewer portion of the bill (since leaked water never entered the sewer) and sometimes a portion of the water charges above your normal usage. The key qualifier: the leak must be repaired. Utilities don't adjust bills for ongoing leaks — the program rewards fixing the problem, not ignoring it. Check your specific provider's website for their leak adjustment policy; search for "leak adjustment" plus your utility's name. The policy page will spell out the eligibility window, the documentation required, and whether there's a cap on the credit.

The Documentation You Need

This is where most claims succeed or fail, so gather it before you file. You'll need: the repair invoice from a licensed plumber showing what was fixed and when (this is the single most important document — a DIY fix with no paperwork is much harder to get credited); before-and-after water meter readings if you have them; the billing periods affected; and photos of the leak or the repair if available. If we did your repair, ask us for an itemized invoice that explicitly states "leak repair" and the date completed — we write these for adjustment claims regularly and know what utilities want to see. Keep copies of everything you submit; claims occasionally need a follow-up call.

How to File the Claim

The process is refreshingly simple. Most utilities have an online leak adjustment form — fill it out with your account number, the billing periods affected, a description of the leak and the repair, and upload your plumber's invoice. Some still want a phone call or a mailed form; the policy page will say. File promptly: many utilities require the claim within 60 to 90 days of the repair, and late filings get denied regardless of merit. After submitting, expect a few weeks for processing. The credit usually appears as a line item on a future bill rather than a refund check. If your claim is denied, call and ask why — sometimes it's a missing document you can supply, not a final answer.

What Adjustments Typically Cover (and Don't)

Set expectations honestly. Adjustments generally credit the excess usage above your historical average — not the entire bill. Sewer charges on leaked water are the most commonly forgiven, since that water never reached the treatment plant. Some utilities cap the credit or limit how often you can claim. What won't be adjusted: bills from leaks you knew about and didn't fix for months (that's considered negligence), irrigation overuse that wasn't a leak, and mysterious high usage with no documented repair. The program is designed for genuine surprises, not chronic waste. The best strategy remains the boring one: fix leaks fast, keep your invoice, and file quickly. At Redmond Flow Plumbing, we flag unusually high usage to homeowners when we spot it — catching a silent toilet leak early can save you hundreds before the bill even arrives.

Preventing the Next Surprise Bill

The cheapest adjustment is the one you never need. Build two habits. First, read your water meter once a month — it takes thirty seconds, and a creeping increase between readings catches silent leaks (running toilets, dripping irrigation valves, slab leaks) before they become four-figure bills. Many utilities now offer online usage portals with daily data; a spike on a day nobody was home is a leak's fingerprint. Second, do a twice-yearly walkthrough: check under every sink with a flashlight, look at your water heater's base, listen for toilets that refill on their own, and walk your irrigation zones while they run. A $10 toilet flapper replaced today is a $400 bill you never receive. And if your home is older, consider a whole-house leak detection shutoff device — it watches flow patterns and closes the main automatically when something looks wrong.
